Barcode and Label Films: Clarity, Adhesion and Scan Reliability

A label is a small surface with a large failure cost. How to specify a film that keeps its adhesive bond and its scan rate in real warehouse conditions.

Applications 2026-09-28 7 min read

Key points

  • Adhesion depends on surface energy, not on film strength
  • Polyester gives dimensional stability for high-speed registration
  • Specify the label stock environment: cold, hot, wet or oily
  • Barcode readability depends on contrast and print density, not only on resolution

Labels fail in three characteristic ways. The adhesive lifts at the edges and the label curls away from the bottle. The film stretches during application and the registration against the print on the container drifts. Or the barcode scans intermittently because the print density is inconsistent. Each has a different cause, and each is decided when the film is specified.

Adhesion is a surface-energy question

Adhesive wetting depends on the surface energy of the label face film. Polypropylene and polyethylene naturally have low surface energy, so they need treatment or a tie-layer to hold a conventional acrylic adhesive; untreated versions will lift. Polyesters accept adhesive more readily, and surface-treated polyester is a dependable choice where long-term bond strength matters. If your label faces a stress-sensitive pack - a curved bottle, a flexible pouch - specify a stretch-oriented or shrink label grade rather than a flat film, because flat film will lift at the corners.

Registration and dimensional stability

High-speed labelling places the label while the container is moving. A label film that stretches or takes up moisture changes size between the print stage and the application stage, and the registration drifts. Biaxially oriented polyester has very low elongation and excellent dimensional stability, which is why it is the standard for high-speed labelling of rigid containers. For moisture-exposed environments, film with a good moisture barrier avoids the label shrinking on the pack.

Describe the environment, not the label

The single most useful sentence in a label specification describes the environment: chilled or ambient, hot-fill or room temperature, dry or wet, and whether the pack contains oil or alcohol. Cold storage, a hot-fill line and a freezer each impose a different requirement on the adhesive and on the film. A supplier who knows the environment can recommend a system; a specification that only lists thickness and colour cannot.

Barcode readability

A code scans when there is enough contrast between bar and space and enough total ink density. Uncoated stock with a matte surface scatters light and lowers contrast. Gloss or semi-gloss faces reproduce the print more faithfully. Very high resolution is not the answer to a scanning problem; contrast and density are. If codes must be read by warehouse scanners through a film or a laminate, test the complete pack - codes that read on the label can stop reading once a gloss overlay is added.

Practical specification checklist

For most label work, specify: base polymer and grade, thickness with tolerance, surface and treatment level on the face and on the adhesive side, shrink or stretch behaviour if applicable, colour and gloss level, print method and the ink or toner system, and the environment the label will face. That is enough for a supplier to quote accurately and for you to audit consistently.
